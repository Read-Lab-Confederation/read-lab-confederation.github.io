Favorite Articles 2025–2026

July 24, 2026

Tim Read , Megan Phillips , Anayancy Ramos , Katrina Hofstetter & Parul Sharma

Members of the EMERGENT group were asked to each pick a favorite paper published since the beginning of 2025. Each person wrote a paragraph explaining why they chose the article. These selections highlight recent advancements in genomics, infectious diseases, and evolutionary biology.

Tim: The spatiotemporal distribution of human pathogens in ancient Eurasia

Sikora M, Canteri E, Fernandez-Guerra A, Oskolkov N, Ågren R, Hansson L, et al. The spatiotemporal distribution of human pathogens in ancient Eurasia. Nature 2025:1–9. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09192-8.

“This paper is the intersection of many subjects that fascinate me: genomics, infectious diseases, history, archaeology, ancient DNA. The authors systematically searched 1,313 ancient Eurasian human sequencing projects for traces of ancient pathogen DNA, finding 492 species. In addition to the analysis of the history of the individual species that will be spurred by this work, the data paints the big picture of a higher infectious disease burden in humans starting about 6,000 years ago, coincident with the shift from hunter-gatherer to pastoral lifestyle. I would have loved to have done this work!”

Megan: Multiple ecological mechanisms drive treatment-induced antibiotic resistance

Shepherd MJ, Harrington NE, Kotarra A, Igler C, Cagney K, Fu T, et al. Multiple ecological and evolutionary mechanisms drive treatment-induced antibiotic resistance. bioRxiv 2026:2026.02.18.705331. https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.02.18.705331.

“To better understand the within-patient dynamics shaping the emergence of antimicrobial resistance, the authors analyze nearly 25,000 isolates from 180 patients with chronic lung infections enrolled in an antibiotic clinical trial. I chose this paper because it investigates the eco-evolutionary mechanisms proposed in previous theoretical and clinical work at a larger scale in real patient data over time. They find diverse resistance trajectories, even in people infected with the same species of bacteria in the same body site and treated with the same antibiotic. The results of this paper indicate that it may be in the interest of patients and public health to switch antibiotics once resistance emerges and to engage in more personalized antibiotic use with the help of evolutionary theory.”

Ana: Genomic comparison and phenotypic characterization of Pseudomonas aeruginosa across environmental and diverse clinical isolation sites

Penaranda C, Brenner EP, Clatworthy AE, Cosimi LA, Ravi J, Hung DT. Genomic comparison and phenotypic characterization of Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates across environmental and diverse clinical isolation sites. mSystems 2026;11:e0136225. https://doi.org/10.1128/msystems.01362-25.

“This paper ties in closely with my own research, conceptually. They ran four virulence assays — cytotoxicity (how well a strain kills human cells), swimming motility, biofilm formation, and pyocyanin production — then used a bacterial GWAS to ask which genes are associated with each trait (antibiotic resistance was tested too, but handled separately, not through the GWAS). Of the four, only cytotoxicity gave a clean answer: it tracked whether a strain carries exoU, a Type III secretion effector. What I find genuinely useful is the scale this worked at: 128 strains. Human GWAS usually needs thousands of genomes, because most human traits are shaped by many genes that each nudge the outcome only a tiny bit, so you need a huge sample to catch each small effect. On the bacterial side, cytotoxicity is the opposite: it rides on one common, large-effect, present-or-absent gene (exoU is carried by roughly a quarter of the strains), tested as simple gene presence/absence across the pangenome with a phylogeny-aware method that makes it harder for a shared lineage to fake the signal. Big effect plus high frequency plus a small search space means it’s detectable even at this scale. This also suggests that the traits that gave no hit (motility, biofilm, and pyocyanin) are multifactorial, with many genes and heavy regulation, and those would likely need thousands of strains.”

Katrina: Large-scale characterisation of the nasal microbiome redefines Staphylococcus aureus colonisation status

Aggarwal D, Bellis KL, Blane B, de Goffau MC, Wagner J, Ng DYK, et al. Large-scale characterisation of the nasal microbiome redefines Staphylococcus aureus colonisation status. Nat Commun 2025;16:10415. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-66564-4.

“The main question of this paper was whether Community State Types (CST) of the nasal microbiome would be associated with persistent, intermediate, or non-carriers of S. aureus.

A huge strength of this paper is found in the design of the experiment. Instead of using a cohort of patients from a hospital setting, Aggarwal et al. recruited 1,180 healthy individuals from England to submit weekly nasal swabs for microbiome profiling. This approach yields a more accurate depiction of S. aureus colonization in the general population and avoids over-representing S. aureus clonal complex types associated with hospital settings. Aggarwal et al. were able to identify CSTs that were both associated with S. aureus persistence carriage and non-carriers. Some bacteria identified in the non-carrier CSTs (Corynebacterium and Dolosigranulum) have been shown by other groups to inhibit S. aureus colonization. The authors were also able to create a model that predicted persistent S. aureus carriage with high sensitivity and specificity directly from microbiome composition data.”

Parul: Genomic resistance in historical clinical isolates increased in frequency and mobility after the age of antibiotics

Kaul A, Souque C, Holland M, Baym M. Genomic resistance in historical clinical isolates increased in frequency and mobility after the age of antibiotics. Microb Genom 2025;11. https://doi.org/10.1099/mgen.0.001474.

“This study examines antimicrobial resistance elements in historical bacterial isolates. Using genome sequencing and comparative genomic analyses, the authors showed that several resistance-associated elements were already present in bacterial populations before the widespread clinical use of antibiotics. The paper focused on the evolutionary context of resistance elements and how their prevalence changed following the use of antibiotics.

What I found particularly interesting was the discussion around the idea that many ‘resistance genes’ may not have originally evolved for antibiotic resistance at all. The paper highlights examples such as efflux pumps, which likely evolved for broader physiological functions including transport and protection against natural environmental stressors. From this perspective, the presence of these genes in pre-antibiotic-era isolates is not necessarily surprising. Instead, the key driver of today’s widespread antimicrobial resistance appears to be selective pressure. Antibiotics transformed these once-rare elements into highly prevalent and mobile features of bacterial populations. I thought the paper did an excellent job of framing antimicrobial resistance as an evolutionary and ecological phenomenon, rather than only a clinical one.”
